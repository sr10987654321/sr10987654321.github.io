Room 8 Maths
WORD PROBLEMS

Finding the maths hiding inside the story

Today we are not hunting for random numbers. We are working out what is happening, what the question wants, and which information actually matters.

Goal: Turn a word problem into a multiplication equation, then solve it accurately.
The routine

Read it like a detective, not a calculator.

1
Read the whole problem.
Do not grab the first two numbers you see.
2
Find the question.
What are we actually trying to work out?
3
Spot the equal groups.
How many groups? How many in each group?
4
Ignore information that does not help.
Some details are there to distract you.
5
Write the equation first.
For example: 126 × 7 = ?
6
Estimate, solve, check.
Your answer should make sense in the story.
Press Space / → to reveal each step
Example 1 · Find the structure
Mr Richmondson's mysterious cookie machine makes 126 cookies in each batch. During a suspiciously good maths lesson, it makes 7 batches. How many cookies does it make altogether?

What is happening?

There are 7 equal groups.

Each group has 126 cookies.

equal groups
“each batch”

What do we need to find?

The total number of cookies.

126 × 7 = ?
Example 1 · Solve and check

Before calculating, estimate.

Estimate

126 is close to 100.

100 × 7 = 700

So the real answer should be a bit more than 700.

Calculate

126 × 7 = 882

Check: 882 is a bit more than 700, so it is reasonable.

Example 2 · Beware the extra detail
Room 8 owns 9 ponchos, 3 missing clickers and an unreasonable number of sticks. For the school fair, students make 8 boxes with 214 raffle tickets in each box. How many raffle tickets are there altogether?

Useful information

8 boxes

214 tickets in each box

8 groups
214 in each

Distracting information

The ponchos and clickers make the story fun, but they do not help answer the question.

214 × 8 = ?
Example 3 · Which equation?
Osha the RAC Arena ghost whispers “Waaattteerrr…” exactly 134 times each night. She haunts the arena for 6 nights. How many ghostly whispers are heard altogether?
134 + 6 = ?
134 × 6 = ?
134 ÷ 6 = ?

Why multiplication?

134 happens again and again for 6 equal nights.

That is repeated equal groups.

6 groups
134 in each group
Example 4 · Harder wording
Smichael is organising the evidence from the grand piano security cameras. There are 243 video clips on each tablet, and he has 4 tablets. He has already watched 12 clips, but how many clips were stored on the tablets before he watched any?

What matters?

4 tablets

243 clips on each tablet

What is a trap?

The 12 watched clips.

The question asks how many were there before he watched any.

243 × 4 = ?
Example 5 · Multi-step challenge
Floisin sharpens 132 pencils for each of 5 tables because apparently normal stationery is not dramatic enough. Then 24 pencils are taken to another classroom. How many pencils remain in Room 8?

Step 1: Find the total

132 × 5 = 660

Step 2: Use the new information

660 − 24 = 636

Answer: 636 pencils remain.

Notice: Multiplication came first because we needed the total number of pencils before we could subtract.
Your turn · Talk before calculating
The class is making Royal Show showbags. There are 7 teams, and each team makes 185 pretend prizes. The teacher also has 6 rolls of sticky tape. How many pretend prizes are made altogether?

1. Question?

How many pretend prizes altogether?

2. Useful numbers?

7 teams and 185 prizes in each team.

3. Equation?

185 × 7 = ?
The Room 8 checklist

When a word problem looks messy, do this.

1 Read the whole story.
2 Box the actual question.
3 Underline the useful numbers.
4 Ask: “Are there equal groups?”
5 Write the equation before solving.
6 Estimate → calculate → check.
Best habit: Explain why your equation matches the story. If you can explain it, you probably understand it.
